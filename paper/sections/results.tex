\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report five results: when the answer becomes readable (\ref{sec:step}), whether places are shared across different questions (\ref{sec:shared}), whether the model uses places (\ref{sec:steer}), what places reveal about wrong answers (\ref{sec:errors}), and whether places follow the given or the true answer (\ref{sec:given}). Table~\ref{tab:models} collects the main numbers for each model.

\subsection{When the answer becomes readable}
\label{sec:step}

Figure~\ref{fig:read}A shows readability layer by layer. In every model the curve has the same shape: a low plateau, a jump, and a high plateau. The jump takes a single layer. In Llama-3.1-8B, readability rises from \JumpFromLlama{} at layer \JumpLayerBeforeLlama{} to \JumpToLlama{} at layer \OnsetLayerLlama{}. The other three models show the same one-layer jump (Table~\ref{tab:models}). After the jump, readability keeps rising to a peak of \PeakLlamaSmall{}--\PeakLlama{}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_readability.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Readability by depth.} (A) All four models, with the two controls of Section~\ref{sec:controls} (shown for Llama-3.1-8B). (B) Llama-3.1-8B under the three tests of Section~\ref{sec:shared}; the standard, other-wording and answer-words-removed curves nearly coincide. Bands are 95\% intervals.}
\label{fig:read}
\end{figure*}

We call the layer of the jump the \emph{onset layer}, and its depth the \emph{onset depth}. The onset depth differs between models, from \OnsetMin{} to \OnsetMax{} of the way through the network. We find the onset layer without looking at test questions: we split the training subjects in half again, build places from one half, measure readability on the other, and take the first layer at which this readability covers half the distance from chance to its highest value over layers. Chance here is the average, over questions, of one over the number of places of the question's kind.

Both controls stay low at every layer, in every model: \ShuffledMin{}--\ShuffledMax{} with shuffled answers and \TextMin{}--\TextMax{} with the question text only. Before the jump, readability is \EarlyMin{}--\EarlyMax{}, above both controls. This early signal may come from surface cues in the subject's name, such as its language; we did not test this. It does not grow toward the jump. The jump is smaller for sports, the one kind of answer that is not a name (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

Tightness shows the same event without whitening (Figure~\ref{fig:tight}). From its highest to its lowest value, it falls \TightFoldMin{}- to \TightFoldMax{}-fold, and the fall is concentrated at the onset. Two of each model's 16 to 42 layers, the one into the onset layer and the one out of it, carry \TightAtJumpMin{}--\TightAtJumpMax{} of the whole fall (on a log scale). Tightness then keeps falling more slowly for about ten layers. This differs from trained classifiers, in which the classes separate by a roughly constant factor at every layer~\citep{he2022a}.

\subsection{Places are shared across questions}
\label{sec:shared}

If a place stands for the answer itself, it should not depend on what exactly the question asks or how. We test this in three ways, each a change to the standard readability measurement (Figure~\ref{fig:read}B, Table~\ref{tab:models}). In each test, a drop in readability would mean that places depend on the thing changed.

\paragraph{Other relations.} We build places from the training questions of one relation only, for example citizenship, and measure readability on test questions of the other relations with the same kind of answer, for example the countries of towns and films. We do this for each relation in turn and average the results. Sports, with a single relation, are left out of this test. In Llama-3.1-8B, readability at 90\% of depth is \XRelLlama{}, against \NamesLateLlama{} in the standard measurement on the same kinds of answer, in which places are built from all relations. The place of a country is therefore much the same whether the question is about a person, a town or a film.

\paragraph{Other wordings.} We build places from plain questions only and measure readability on the rewordings. Readability is \XWordLlama{}, the same as in the standard measurement.

\paragraph{Answer words removed.} A place could simply be the model preparing to say a particular word, since that would also be shared across questions. To test this, we removed from every state the directions through which the model writes the answer words. The model produces a word by comparing its final state with one fixed vector per token (its unembedding vector), after a normalisation that rescales each dimension. We took these vectors, rescaled in the same way, for every token of the 62 answer names, and removed from each state every direction they span, keeping only the part of the state at right angles to them. Readability did not change (\NoWordsLlama{}). Removing the same number of random directions did not change it either. Places can therefore be read without the directions that write the answer word.

\subsection{The model uses places}
\label{sec:steer}

Readability shows that the answer can be read from the state. It does not show that the model uses this information. To test that, we moved states into places and checked whether the answer changed. For each test question that the model had answered wrongly, with an answer that has a place (\PushNMin{}--\PushNMax{} questions per model), we changed its state at one layer to
\begin{equation}
h' = h + \left(p_{\text{right}} - p_{\text{given}}\right),
\label{eq:move}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item $h$ is the question's state at that layer, at the last prompt position;
\item $p_{\text{given}}$ is the place of the wrong answer the model gave;
\item $p_{\text{right}}$ is the place of the right answer;
\item $h'$ is the moved state.
\end{itemize}
The move therefore shifts the state by exactly the gap between the two places. The move is made once, while the model reads the prompt; the model then generates its reply without further changes. We made this move at one depth at a time, at seven depths: 20\%, 35\%, 50\%, 60\%, 70\%, 80\% and 90\% of the network. We also ran two controls: a move of the same length in a random direction, and a move toward the place of a third answer, neither given nor right, drawn at random from the places of the same kind. Finally, we took questions the model had answered correctly and moved them toward the place of another answer of the same kind, also drawn at random.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_steering.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Moving states toward places} (Section~\ref{sec:steer}). (A) Wrong answers moved toward the right answer's place. (B) Right answers moved toward another answer's place. Solid lines: moves toward the place; dotted lines: random moves of the same length. Short bars under each axis mark each model's onset depth.}
\label{fig:steer}
\end{figure*}

Moves work only after the onset (Figure~\ref{fig:steer}). At 20\% of depth they correct \PushEarlyMin{}--\PushEarlyMax{} of wrong answers. After the onset, the later the move, the more often it works. At about 90\% of depth it corrects \PushGoldLlama{} of wrong answers in Llama-3.1-8B, \PushGoldQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B, \PushGoldGemma{} in Gemma-2-9B and \PushGoldLlamaSmall{} in Llama-3.2-1B. Random moves of the same length produce the right answer in only \PushRandomMin{}--\PushRandomMax{} of cases.

The effect is not specific to the right answer. At 90\% of depth, wrong answers moved toward a third answer's place give that answer in \PushOtherMin{}--\PushOtherMax{} of cases, and right answers moved toward another answer's place switch to it in \PushRightOtherMin{}--\PushRightOtherMax{} of cases.

One caveat concerns size. Because places move apart with depth, the moves grow from \PushRelEarlyMin{}--\PushRelEarlyMax{} of the state's length at 20\% of depth to \PushRelLateMin{}--\PushRelLateMax{} at 90\%. Late moves are large and close to the output, and may partly write the answer directly. The clearest evidence is therefore the depth at which moves start to work, and the failure of random moves of the same length.

\subsection{Wrong answers: near misses and gaps}
\label{sec:errors}

Because places are built from the answers the model gives, a wrong answer has a place too, and we can ask what the state of a wrong answer looks like. Some wrong answers are \emph{near misses}: the model fails the open question but picks the right option when the same question is asked as multiple choice. We count a wrong answer as a near miss only if the model picks the right option in at least three of the four option orders. This holds for \ErrPosMin{}--\ErrPosMax{} of wrong answers, fewest in Llama-3.2-1B. The other wrong answers we call \emph{gaps}.

Can near misses be told apart from gaps? We compared two single numbers for each wrong answer to a plain test question whose right answer has a place:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item \emph{Rank of the right place.} At the onset layer, where answers first become readable, we rank all places of the question's kind by their distance from the state (in whitened states), with 1 for the nearest, and take the rank of the right answer's place.
\item \emph{Confidence.} The model's average log-probability per token of its own reply.
\end{itemize}
We measure how well each number separates near misses from gaps with the AUC: the probability that a randomly chosen near miss scores better than a randomly chosen gap (a nearer right place, or a more confident reply). An AUC of 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect separation. Both AUCs use the same wrong answers, and neither number is fitted to the data.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_recognition.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Telling near misses from gaps} (Section~\ref{sec:errors}). AUC of each single number among wrong answers; 0.5 is chance.}
\label{fig:errors}
\end{figure}

The rank of the right place separates near misses from gaps better than confidence in every model (Figure~\ref{fig:errors}): AUC \AucRankMin{}--\AucRankMax{}, against \AucConfMeanMin{}--\AucConfMeanMax{} for confidence. The 95\% interval for the difference excludes zero in every model. For near misses, the right answer's place has a median rank of \RankRecogMin{}--\RankRecogMax{}, depending on the model; for gaps it is \RankNotMin{}--\RankNotMax{}. A near miss is thus visible inside the model: the right answer's place is among the nearest, often just behind the place of the answer the model gave. The model's confidence in its reply barely shows this.

\subsection{Places follow the given answer}
\label{sec:given}

Places are built from the answers the model gives. Two results show that this is also what they represent, rather than the true answer. First, take two models, M and N, and the plain test questions to which they give different answers. On these questions, M's state at its onset layer lies nearer M's place for M's answer than M's place for N's answer in \CrossModelMin{}--\CrossModelMax{} of cases (the range over all \CrossModelPairs{} ordered pairs of models). Second, we built places from the true answers instead, and measured how often the nearest place is the true answer. At 90\% of depth this readability is \GoldLateMin{}--\GoldLateMax{}, well below the \GivenLateMin{}--\GivenLateMax{} of places built from the given answers (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
