\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Onset of answer readability}
\label{sec:step}

Figure~\ref{fig:read}A shows readability layer by layer. In every model the curve has the same shape: a low plateau, a jump, and a high plateau. The jump takes a single layer. In Llama-3.1-8B, readability rises from \JumpFromLlama{} at layer \JumpLayerBeforeLlama{} to \JumpToLlama{} at layer \OnsetLayerLlama{}. The other models show the same one-layer jump (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Readability then keeps rising, to a peak of \PeakLlamaSmall{}--\PeakLlama{}.

We call the depth of the jump the \emph{onset depth}. It differs between models, from \OnsetMin{} to \OnsetMax{} of the way through the network (Table~\ref{tab:models}). To choose it without looking at test questions, we split the training subjects in half again, build places from one half, measure readability on the other, and take the first layer at which readability covers half the distance from chance to its peak.

Before the jump, \EarlyMin{}--\EarlyMax{} of answers can already be read, more than with shuffled answers (\ShuffledLlama{}) or from the question's characters alone (\TextLlama{}--\TextQwen{}). This early signal may come from surface cues in the subject's name, such as its language; we did not test this. It does not grow toward the jump.

\paragraph{Tightness.} Readability uses whitened states, so the jump could in principle come from the whitening. Tightness uses the model's own states and shows the same event. It falls \TightFoldMin{}- to \TightFoldMax{}-fold through the network (Figure~\ref{fig:tight}). The fall is steepest at the onset: the drop from the layer before the onset layer to the layer after it is \TightAtJumpMin{}--\TightAtJumpMax{} of the whole fall in log tightness. After that, tightness keeps falling more slowly for about ten layers.

Is this fall a step or a steady contraction? We fitted two curves to log tightness as a function of depth. A straight line means the same contraction at every layer, the pattern found in trained classifiers~\citep{he2022a}. A smooth step (a logistic curve) means a sudden change. To compare them fairly, we left out one layer at a time, fitted each curve to the remaining layers, and measured the squared error in predicting the left-out layer. The step predicts better in every model, with \StepRatioMin{} to \StepRatioMax{} times less error (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The step is not instantaneous, though: the fitted curve goes from 10\% to 90\% of its drop over \StepRiseMin{} to \StepRiseMax{} of the network's depth.

\subsection{Transfer across relations, wordings and formats}
\label{sec:shared}

A place is shared by very different questions. To test this, we built places from the training questions of one relation only, for example people's citizenship, and measured readability on test questions of the other relations with the same kind of answer, for example the country of a town or a film. At 90\% of depth this cross-relation readability is \XRelLlama{} in Llama-3.1-8B, against \GivenLateLlama{} within the same relation (Figure~\ref{fig:read}B). Likewise, places built from plain questions identify the answers to rewordings just as well (\XWordLlama{}). A place therefore does not depend on how the question is asked.

A place could still be just the model's plan to say a particular word, since such a plan would also be shared across questions. One test speaks to this. To produce a word, the model scores each token by the dot product of its final state with a fixed vector for that token, its unembedding vector, after a normalisation that rescales each dimension. These vectors, rescaled the same way, are the directions through which the state writes words. We took them for every token of the 62 answer names and removed all the directions they span from every state, keeping only the part of each state at right angles to them. Readability did not change (\NoWordsLlama{} at 90\% of depth). Removing a random set of directions of the same number did not change it either. Reading a place therefore does not need the directions that write the answer; the place is also present in other directions.

The multiple-choice format shows where sharing stops. Here the model answers with a letter. We asked whether a multiple-choice state lies nearest the free-form place of the option the model chose. Because the two prompt formats differ, all multiple-choice states are shifted relative to free-form states. We estimated this shift as the difference between the two formats' average training states and subtracted it. At the onset depth, the multiple-choice state briefly does lie nearest the chosen option's place: \McqPeakLlama{} of the time in Llama-3.1-8B, against \ShuffledLlama{} with shuffled answers. It then falls to \ToMcqLlama{} as the state turns toward a letter. This fits the finding that models choose the content of an option before binding it to its letter~\citep{wong2026when}.

\subsection{Interventions on the state}
\label{sec:steer}

Readability does not show that the model uses a state's position. To test this, we moved states and checked whether the answer changed. We took test questions that the model had answered wrongly, with an answer that has a place (\PushNMin{}--\PushNMax{} questions per model). At one layer, and only at the last prompt position, we replaced the state $h$ with $h + (p_{\text{right}} - p_{\text{given}})$, the state moved by the vector from the given answer's place to the right answer's place. The model then continued its computation and generated its reply as usual. We ran two controls. One moves the state by a vector of the same length in a random direction. The other moves the state toward the place of a third answer, neither given nor right. We repeated this at seven depths between 20\% and 90\% of the network.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_steering.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Moving the state toward an answer's place, by the depth of the move.} (A) Wrong answers moved toward the right answer's place (solid) or in a random direction of the same length (dotted). (B) Right answers moved toward another answer's place (solid) or in a random direction of the same length (dotted). The y-axis is the share of questions for which the model then gives the answer it was moved toward. Short bars below each axis mark each model's onset depth. Test questions; places from training questions.}
\label{fig:steer}
\end{figure*}

Moves work only after the onset depth (Figure~\ref{fig:steer}). At 20\% of depth they correct \PushEarlyMin{}--\PushEarlyMax{} of wrong answers. After the onset, the later the move, the better it works. At about 90\% of depth they correct \PushGoldLlama{} of wrong answers in Llama-3.1-8B, \PushGoldQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B, \PushGoldGemma{} in Gemma-2-9B and \PushGoldLlamaSmall{} in Llama-3.2-1B. Random moves of the same length produce the right answer in only \PushRandomMin{}--\PushRandomMax{} of cases.

The effect is not specific to the right answer. Moved toward a third answer's place, the model gives that answer in \PushOtherMin{}--\PushOtherMax{} of cases. Questions the model had answered correctly, moved toward another answer's place, switch to that answer in \PushRightOtherMin{}--\PushRightOtherMax{} of cases. Some wrong answers have no place of their own, mostly because they name something outside the candidate list. For these, we used the average state of all training questions in place of $p_{\text{given}}$. They are corrected less often (\PushUnplacedMin{}--\PushUnplacedMax{} at 90\% of depth, \PushNUnplacedMin{}--\PushNUnplacedMax{} questions per model).

Because places move apart with depth, the moves grow from \PushRelEarlyMin{}--\PushRelEarlyMax{} of the state's length at 20\% of depth to \PushRelLateMin{}--\PushRelLateMax{} at 90\%. Late moves are large and close to the output, and may partly write the answer directly. The more telling results are the depth at which moves start to work and the failure of random moves.

\subsection{Wrong answers}
\label{sec:errors}

Because places are built from the answers the model gives, a wrong answer lands in a place too. At the onset depth, \AnatInGivenLlama{} of Llama-3.1-8B's wrong answers that name a candidate lie nearest the place of the answer it gave (\AnatInGivenQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B). In Llama-3.1-8B, \AnatInGoldLlama{} of all wrong answers lie nearest the right answer's place instead.

Some wrong answers are near misses: asked the same question as multiple choice, the model picks the right option. We call these wrong answers \emph{recognised}, and we define the term strictly: the model must pick the right option in at least three of the four option orders. This holds for \ErrPosMin{}--\ErrPosMax{} of wrong answers, fewest in Llama-3.2-1B.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_recognition.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Telling near misses from real gaps.} For wrong free-form answers to test questions: how well each set of signals predicts that the model still picks the right option in multiple choice. Confidence: the reply's log-probability and length. Geometry: where the state lies relative to places at the onset depth. AUC on test questions (Section~\ref{sec:errors}); 0.5 is chance.}
\label{fig:errors}
\end{figure}

Can these near misses be told apart from real gaps in knowledge? We compared two sets of signals. The first is the model's confidence in its wrong reply: the reply's log-probability and its length. The second is the geometry of the state at the onset layer, measured in whitened states: its distance to the right answer's place, the rank of that place among all places by distance, and how much nearer the nearest place is than the second nearest. For each set, we trained a logistic regression to predict recognition. We scored it by AUC, the probability that a recognised wrong answer gets a higher predicted score than an unrecognised one (0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect). The regression must be scored on wrong answers it was not fitted on, so we used five-fold cross-validation: we split the wrong answers into five groups, keeping each subject in a single group, trained on four groups, predicted the fifth, and repeated this for every group.

Geometry predicts recognition better than confidence in every model (Figure~\ref{fig:errors}). In Llama-3.1-8B, the AUC is \AucGeoLlama{} for geometry against \AucConfLlama{} for confidence. In Gemma-2-9B it is \AucGeoGemma{} against \AucConfGemma{}, so Gemma's confidence carries no signal here at all. Adding geometry to confidence raises AUC in every model, and the 95\% interval for the gain, from resampling subjects, excludes zero in each (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

The rank of the right answer's place shows what the geometry sees. Rank all places by their distance from the state, with 1 for the nearest. For recognised wrong answers, the right answer's place has a median rank of \RankRecogMin{}--\RankRecogMax{}, depending on the model. For unrecognised ones, its median rank is \RankNotMin{}--\RankNotMax{}. So a near miss is visible inside the model: the right answer's place is among the nearest, often just behind the place of the answer the model gave. The model's confidence in its wrong reply does not show this.

A wrong suggestion sometimes makes a model give a wrong answer to a question it otherwise answers correctly (Figure~\ref{fig:hint}). When the model keeps the right answer, its state goes to the right place and stays there: at the last layer of Llama-3.1-8B, \HintResistEndLlama{} of these states lie nearer the right place than the suggested one. When Llama-3.1-8B gives the wrong answer, its state first moves toward the right place at the onset depth (\HintPeakLlama{} nearer it at layer \OnsetLayerLlama{}). It then drifts back toward the suggestion (\HintEndLlama{} at the last layer; \HintMisledNLlama{} questions). The other models also drift away from the right place late in the network, but show no clear early approach to it.

\subsection{Given versus true answers}
\label{sec:given}

Places follow the answer a model gives, not the true answer. When two models give different answers to the same question, each model's state lies nearer the place of its own answer than the place of the other model's answer (both places from that model) in \CrossModelMin{}--\CrossModelMax{} of cases, across all \CrossModelPairs{} ordered pairs of models. And places built from the given answers identify those answers far better than places built from the true answers identify the true answers. At 90\% of depth, readability is \GivenLateLlama{} against \GoldLateLlama{} in Llama-3.1-8B, and \GivenLateQwen{} against \GoldLateQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B.
