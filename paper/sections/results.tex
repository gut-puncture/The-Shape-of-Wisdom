\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Onset of answer readability}
\label{sec:step}

Figure~\ref{fig:read}A shows readability layer by layer. In every model the curve has the same shape: a low plateau, a jump, and a high plateau. The jump takes a single layer. In Llama-3.1-8B, readability rises from \JumpFromLlama{} at layer \JumpLayerBeforeLlama{} to \JumpToLlama{} at layer \OnsetLayerLlama{}. The other models show the same one-layer jump (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Readability then keeps rising, to a peak of \PeakLlamaSmall{}--\PeakLlama{}.

We call the depth of the jump the \emph{onset depth}. It differs between models, from \OnsetMin{} to \OnsetMax{} of the way through the network (Table~\ref{tab:models}). We locate it with training subjects only, as the first layer at which readability covers half the distance from chance to its peak.

(B) Transfer tests in Llama-3.1-8B. ``Other relation'': places fitted on one relation and tested on the others. ``Other wording'': places fitted on plain questions and tested on rewordings. ``Answer words removed'': the directions the model uses to write the answer names are removed from the state. ``Multiple choice'': places tested on the states of the multiple-choice questions. The same-relation, other-wording and answer-words-removed curves nearly coincide.}
\label{fig:read}

Before the jump, \EarlyMin{}--\EarlyMax{} of answers can already be read, more than with shuffled answers (\ShuffledLlama{}) or from the question's characters alone (\TextLlama{}--\TextQwen{}). This early signal may come from surface cues in the subject's name, such as its language; we did not test this. It does not grow toward the jump.

\paragraph{Tightness.} Readability uses whitened states, so the jump could in principle come from the whitening. Tightness uses the model's own states and shows the same event. It falls \TightFoldMin{}- to \TightFoldMax{}-fold through the network (Figure~\ref{fig:tight}). The fall is steepest at the onset depth: the two layers around it account for \TightAtJumpMin{}--\TightAtJumpMax{} of the whole fall in log tightness. After that, tightness keeps falling more slowly for about ten layers.

Is this fall a step or a steady contraction? We fitted two shapes to log tightness as a function of depth. A straight line means the same contraction at every layer, the pattern found in trained classifiers~\citep{he2022a}. A smooth step means a sudden change. We compared the two by how well each predicts a layer left out of the fit. The step predicts better in every model, with \StepRatioMin{} to \StepRatioMax{} times less error (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The step is not instantaneous, though: its fitted rise spans \StepRiseMin{} to \StepRiseMax{} of the network's depth.

\subsection{Transfer across relations, wordings and formats}
\label{sec:shared}

A place is shared by very different questions. Places fitted on one relation also read the answers to other relations with the same kind of answer. For example, places fitted on questions about people's citizenship read the country the model gives for a town or a film. At 90\% of depth this cross-relation readability is \XRelLlama{} in Llama-3.1-8B, against \GivenLateLlama{} within the same relation (Figure~\ref{fig:read}B). Places fitted on plain questions read the answers to rewordings just as well (\XWordLlama{}). A place therefore does not depend on how the question is asked.

A place could still be just the model's plan to say a particular word, since such a plan would also be shared across questions. One test speaks to this. The model writes each output token through a fixed direction in the residual stream, its unembedding vector, scaled by the final normalisation. We removed from the state the directions of every token of all 62 answer names. Readability did not change (\NoWordsLlama{} at 90\% of depth). Removing a random set of directions of the same size did not change it either. Reading a place therefore does not need the directions that write the answer; the place is also present in other directions.

The multiple-choice format shows where sharing stops. Here the model answers with a letter. We asked whether a multiple-choice state lies nearest the free-form place of the option the model chose, after removing the average offset between the two formats, fitted on training subjects. At the onset depth it briefly does: \McqPeakLlama{} of the time in Llama-3.1-8B, against \ShuffledLlama{} with shuffled answers. It then falls to \ToMcqLlama{} as the state turns toward a letter. This fits the finding that models choose the content of an option before binding it to its letter~\citep{wong2026when}.

\subsection{Interventions on the state}
\label{sec:steer}

Readability does not show that the model uses a state's position. To test this, we moved states and checked whether the answer changed. We took test questions that the model had answered wrongly, with an answer that has a place (\PushNMin{}--\PushNMax{} questions per model). At one layer, and only at the last prompt position, we added to the state the vector from the given answer's place to the right answer's place. Then we let the model answer. We ran two controls. One adds a random direction of the same length. The other moves the state toward the place of a third answer, neither given nor right. We repeated this at seven depths between 20\% and 90\% of the network.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_steering.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Moving the state toward an answer's place, by the depth of the move.} (A) Wrong answers moved toward the right answer's place (solid) or in a random direction of the same length (dotted). (B) Right answers moved toward another answer's place (solid) or in a random direction of the same length (dotted). The y-axis is the share of questions for which the model then gives the answer it was moved toward. Short bars below each axis mark each model's onset depth. Test questions; places from training subjects.}
\label{fig:steer}
\end{figure*}

Moves work only after the onset depth (Figure~\ref{fig:steer}). At 20\% of depth they correct \PushEarlyMin{}--\PushEarlyMax{} of wrong answers. After the onset, the later the move, the better it works. At about 90\% of depth they correct \PushGoldLlama{} of wrong answers in Llama-3.1-8B, \PushGoldQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B, \PushGoldGemma{} in Gemma-2-9B and \PushGoldLlamaSmall{} in Llama-3.2-1B. Random moves of the same length produce the right answer in only \PushRandomMin{}--\PushRandomMax{} of cases.

The effect is not specific to the right answer. Moved toward a third answer's place, the model gives that answer in \PushOtherMin{}--\PushOtherMax{} of cases. Questions the model had answered correctly, moved toward another answer's place, switch to that answer in \PushRightOtherMin{}--\PushRightOtherMax{} of cases. Some wrong answers name something outside the candidate list and have no place of their own. For these, we moved the state from the average state of all questions instead. They are corrected less often (\PushUnplacedMin{}--\PushUnplacedMax{} at 90\% of depth, \PushNUnplacedMin{}--\PushNUnplacedMax{} questions per model).

Because places move apart with depth, the moves grow from \PushRelEarlyMin{}--\PushRelEarlyMax{} of the state's length at 20\% of depth to \PushRelLateMin{}--\PushRelLateMax{} at 90\%. Late moves are large and close to the output, and may partly write the answer directly. The more telling results are the depth at which moves start to work and the failure of random moves.

\subsection{Wrong answers}
\label{sec:errors}

Because places are built from the answers the model gives, a wrong answer lands in a place too. At the onset depth, \AnatInGivenLlama{} of Llama-3.1-8B's wrong answers that name a candidate lie nearest the place of the answer it gave (\AnatInGivenQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B). In Llama-3.1-8B, \AnatInGoldLlama{} of all wrong answers lie nearest the right answer's place instead.

Some wrong answers are near misses: asked the same question as multiple choice, the model picks the right option. We call these wrong answers \emph{recognised}, and we define the term strictly: the model must pick the right option in at least three of the four option orders. This holds for \ErrPosMin{}--\ErrPosMax{} of wrong answers, fewest in Llama-3.2-1B.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_recognition.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Telling near misses from real gaps.} For wrong free-form answers to test questions: how well each set of signals predicts that the model still picks the right option in multiple choice. Confidence: the reply's log-probability and length. Geometry: where the state lies relative to places at the onset depth. AUC from five-fold cross-validation grouped by subject; 0.5 is chance.}
\label{fig:errors}
\end{figure}

Can these near misses be told apart from real gaps in knowledge? We compared two sets of signals. The first is the model's confidence in its wrong reply: the reply's log-probability and its length. The second is the geometry of the state at the onset depth: its distance to the right answer's place, the rank of that place among all places by distance, and the gap between the two nearest places. For each set, we trained a logistic regression to predict recognition. We scored it by AUC, the probability that a recognised wrong answer gets a higher score than an unrecognised one (0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect). Each subject's questions were kept within one of five cross-validation folds.

Geometry predicts recognition better than confidence in every model (Figure~\ref{fig:errors}). In Llama-3.1-8B, the AUC is \AucGeoLlama{} for geometry against \AucConfLlama{} for confidence. In Gemma-2-9B it is \AucGeoGemma{} against \AucConfGemma{}, so Gemma's confidence carries no signal here at all. Adding geometry to confidence raises AUC in every model, and the 95\% interval for the gain, from resampling subjects, excludes zero in each (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

The rank of the right answer's place shows what the geometry sees. Rank all places by their distance from the state, with 1 for the nearest. For recognised wrong answers, the right answer's place has a median rank of \RankRecogMin{}--\RankRecogMax{}, depending on the model. For unrecognised ones, its median rank is \RankNotMin{}--\RankNotMax{}. So a near miss is visible inside the model: the right answer's place is among the nearest, often just behind the place of the answer the model gave. The model's confidence in its wrong reply does not show this.

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_suggestion.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{States under a wrong suggestion.} Questions that the model answers correctly when asked plainly, asked again with a user's wrong suggestion. Solid: the model then gave a wrong answer. Dashed: it kept the right answer. The y-axis is the share of states that lie nearer the right answer's place than the suggested answer's place.}

A wrong suggestion sometimes makes a model give a wrong answer to a question it otherwise answers correctly (Figure~\ref{fig:hint}). When the model keeps the right answer, its state goes to the right place and stays there: at the last layer of Llama-3.1-8B, \HintResistEndLlama{} of these states lie nearer the right place than the suggested one. When Llama-3.1-8B gives the wrong answer, its state first moves toward the right place at the onset depth (\HintPeakLlama{} nearer it at layer \OnsetLayerLlama{}). It then drifts back toward the suggestion (\HintEndLlama{} at the last layer; \HintMisledNLlama{} questions). The other models also drift away from the right place late in the network, but show no clear early approach to it.

\subsection{Given versus true answers}
\label{sec:given}

Places follow the answer a model gives, not the true answer. When two models give different answers to the same question, each model's state lies nearer its own answer's place than the other model's in \CrossModelMin{}--\CrossModelMax{} of cases, across all \CrossModelPairs{} ordered pairs of models. And places built from the given answers read those answers far better than places built from the true answers read the truth. At 90\% of depth, readability is \GivenLateLlama{} against \GoldLateLlama{} in Llama-3.1-8B, and \GivenLateQwen{} against \GoldLateQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B.
