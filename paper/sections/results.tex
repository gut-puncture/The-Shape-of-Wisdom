\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The answer becomes readable in a single layer}
\label{sec:step}

If the model formed its answer gradually, readability would rise smoothly with depth. It does not. In every model the curve has the same shape: a low plateau, a jump within a single layer, and a high plateau (Figure~\ref{fig:onset}A). In Llama-3.1-8B, readability rises from \JumpFromLlama{} at layer \JumpLayerBeforeLlama{} to \JumpToLlama{} at layer \OnsetLayerLlama{}. The other three models show the same one-layer jump (Table~\ref{tab:models}). After the jump, readability keeps rising to a peak of \PeakLlamaSmall{}--\PeakLlama{}. We call the layer of the jump the \emph{onset layer}: the first layer at which readability covers half the distance from chance to its peak. To choose it without test questions, we measure this readability on training questions, building centroids from one half of the training subjects and testing on the other (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The depth of the onset layer, the \emph{onset depth}, differs between models, from \OnsetMin{} to \OnsetMax{} of the way through the network.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_onset.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Readability and separation by depth.} (A) Readability for the four models. The two horizontal lines are the controls, shown for Llama-3.1-8B; the other models' controls are similar. (B) Separation for the four models, on a log scale. Bands are 95\% intervals.}
\label{fig:onset}
\end{figure*}

Two controls show how much readability our procedure reaches when the state carries no information about the answer. \emph{Shuffled answers} keeps every state but credits each question with the answer of a different, randomly chosen fact of the same kind. Readability then measures pure chance. It is low, \ShuffledMin{}--\ShuffledMax{} across models, because each kind has 10 to 24 candidate answers. \emph{Question text only} replaces the model's state with a vector built from the question's spelling alone, without the model.\footnote{The vector counts every sequence of two to four characters in the question, weighted so that sequences common to many questions count less (term frequency--inverse document frequency, TF-IDF), and is compressed to 256 numbers.} It asks how far the wording gives the answer away, for example a name that sounds Japanese. It reaches \TextMin{}--\TextMax{}. Readability above both lines is information that the model added to the state. Before the jump, readability is already \EarlyMin{}--\EarlyMax{}, above both controls; this early signal may come from cues in the subject's name, which we did not test, and it does not grow toward the jump. The jump is much smaller for sports, the one kind of answer that is not a name: \SportJumpMin{}--\SportJumpMax{} percentage points, against \NamesJumpMin{}--\NamesJumpMax{} for countries, cities and languages (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

Readability depends on the whitening. To check the jump in the model's own states, we also measure \emph{separation}: how far apart the centroids are, compared with how widely questions scatter around their own centroid. At each layer,
\begin{equation}
S = \frac{D_{\text{between}}}{D_{\text{within}}},
\label{eq:tight}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align*}
D_{\text{between}} &= \frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{a \in A} \lVert c_a - \bar{c} \rVert^2, \\
D_{\text{within}} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{q} \lVert h_q - c_{g_q} \rVert^2,
\end{align*}
where
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item $A$ is the set of answers of one kind, $|A|$ is their number, and $\bar{c}$ is the average of their centroids;
\item $D_{\text{between}}$ is therefore the average squared distance of a centroid from the centre of all centroids: how far apart the centroids are;
\item the sum in $D_{\text{within}}$ runs over the $N$ test questions of that kind, and $g_q$ is the answer the model gave to question $q$;
\item $D_{\text{within}}$ is therefore the average squared distance of a question from its own answer's centroid: how widely questions scatter;
\item all distances are measured on the unwhitened states.
\end{itemize}
Higher separation means questions sit closer to their own centroid relative to the distance between centroids. We combine the four kinds of answer by weighting each by its number of test questions.

Separation shows the same event (Figure~\ref{fig:onset}B). From its lowest to its highest value it rises \TightFoldMin{}- to \TightFoldMax{}-fold, and the rise is concentrated at the onset. The two layer steps at the onset, into the onset layer and out of it, carry \TightAtJumpMin{}--\TightAtJumpMax{} of the whole rise on a log scale. If the rise were spread evenly over a model's 16 to 42 layers, two steps would carry only 5\%--13\%. Separation then keeps rising more slowly for about ten layers. This differs from trained classifiers, in which classes separate by a roughly constant factor at every layer~\citep{he2022a}.

\subsection{Centroids follow the model's answer, not the question}
\label{sec:shared}

A centroid is built from questions that share an answer. It could still reflect something else those questions share: the type of fact asked, the wording, or the output word. We test each by changing how the centroids are built or what the state contains, then measuring readability again (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}, Table~\ref{tab:models}). Below, ``standard'' readability is that of Section~\ref{sec:read}; for Llama-3.1-8B it is \GivenLateLlama{} at 90\% of depth.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_transfer.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Readability of Llama-3.1-8B under three changes} (Section~\ref{sec:shared}). The standard, other-wordings and answer-words-removed curves nearly coincide. Bands are 95\% intervals.}
\label{fig:transfer}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Other relations.} If centroids were tied to the type of fact, a country centroid built from questions about people would not identify that country in questions about towns or films. We built centroids from the training questions of one relation only, measured readability on test questions of the other relations with the same kind of answer, and averaged over the choice of relation. (Sports have a single relation and are left out.) In Llama-3.1-8B, readability at 90\% of depth is \XRelLlama{}, against \NamesLateLlama{} with standard centroids on the same kinds of answer. The centroid of a country is nearly the same whether the question is about a person, a town or a film.

\paragraph{Other wordings.} If centroids were tied to the wording, centroids built from plain questions would fail on rewordings such as the casual example in Section~\ref{sec:questions}. We built centroids from plain questions only and measured readability on the rewordings. It is \XWordLlama{}, the same as standard.

\paragraph{Answer words removed.} Every question answered with Argentina ends in the same output word, ``Argentina''. A centroid could simply be that word's output direction. We removed from every state its component along the unembedding vectors of all tokens of all 62 answer names. Then we rebuilt the centroids and measured readability again. It stays at \NoWordsLlama{} in Llama-3.1-8B, and removing the same number of random directions does not change it either. So a centroid is not just the output direction of its word. This does not show that a centroid is more than the model's plan to say that word. A plan formed in the middle of the network need not lie along the output directions, and our tests cannot tell the two apart.

\paragraph{Different models.} When two models answer the same question differently, the state follows each model's own answer. At the onset layer, each model's state lies nearer its own centroid for its own answer than its own centroid for the other model's answer. This holds for \CrossModelMin{}--\CrossModelMax{} of such questions, across all \CrossModelPairs{} ordered pairs of models. As expected, centroids built from the true answers work less well: at 90\% of depth they identify the true answer in \GoldLateMin{}--\GoldLateMax{} of test questions, against \GivenLateMin{}--\GivenLateMax{} for standard centroids and the given answer.

\subsection{The model uses the centroids}
\label{sec:steer}

Readability shows that the answer can be read from the state. It does not show that the model uses this information. If it does, moving a state to another answer's centroid should change the answer. We took the test questions that the model answered wrongly and whose wrong answer has a centroid (\PushNMin{}--\PushNMax{} per model). For each, we changed its state at one layer to
\begin{equation}
h' = h + \left(c_{\text{right}} - c_{\text{given}}\right),
\label{eq:move}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item $h$ is the question's state at that layer;
\item $c_{\text{given}}$ is the centroid of the wrong answer the model gave;
\item $c_{\text{right}}$ is the centroid of the right answer;
\item $h'$ is the moved state, which replaces $h$ while the model reads the prompt; the model then writes its reply as usual.
\end{itemize}
The centroids are the training centroids of that layer, and the move is made in the model's own, unwhitened states. We made this move at one layer at a time, at seven depths from 20\% to 90\% of the network. As controls, we moved the state by the same distance in a random direction, and toward the centroid of a third, randomly chosen answer of the same kind. We also moved right answers toward another answer's centroid.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_steering.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Moving states to answer centroids.} (A) Wrong answers moved to the right answer's centroid. (B) Right answers moved to another answer's centroid. Solid lines: moves to the centroid; dotted lines: random moves of the same length. The y-axis is the share of questions for which the model then gives the target answer. Short bars under each axis mark each model's onset depth.}
\label{fig:steer}
\end{figure*}

Moves work only after the onset (Figure~\ref{fig:steer}). At 20\% of depth they correct \PushEarlyMin{}--\PushEarlyMax{} of wrong answers. After the onset, the later the move, the more often it works. At 90\% of depth, it corrects \PushGoldLlama{} of wrong answers in Llama-3.1-8B, \PushGoldQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B, \PushGoldGemma{} in Gemma-2-9B and \PushGoldLlamaSmall{} in Llama-3.2-1B. Random moves of the same length change the reply in \RandomChangedMin{}--\RandomChangedMax{} of cases, but give the right answer in only \PushRandomMin{}--\PushRandomMax{}. A move this large can disturb the answer; only its direction makes it the right one. The effect is not specific to the truth. At 90\% of depth, moves toward a third answer's centroid produce that answer in \PushOtherMin{}--\PushOtherMax{} of cases, and right answers moved toward another answer's centroid switch to it in \PushRightOtherMin{}--\PushRightOtherMax{}.

\paragraph{Why only after the onset?} One explanation is size. Centroids move apart with depth, so the moves grow from \PushRelEarlyMin{}--\PushRelEarlyMax{} of the state's length at 20\% of depth to \PushRelLateMin{}--\PushRelLateMax{} at 90\%. Early moves might fail only because they are small. We tested this in Llama-3.2-1B (onset layer 10). At layers 3, 6 and 8, we scaled the move from the given to the right centroid up to 17\% and 44\% of the state's length. At the onset layer, a 17\% move corrects \SizeOnsetRight{} of the \SizeN{} wrong answers. Before the onset, a 17\% move corrects at most \SizePreSmallMax{}, and even a 44\% move at most \SizePreLargeMax{}. A random move of 44\% reaches \SizePreRandomMax{}. Size is therefore not the reason. Before the onset, the centroids lie almost on top of each other (Figure~\ref{fig:onset}B), and the difference between two of them carries little information about the answer. Moving along it acts much like a random move. At the onset, that difference becomes a direction the model reads its answer from. Readability and moves are thus two views of one event: the onset is when the answer becomes both readable and steerable.

Late moves are large and close to the output, so they may partly write the answer directly. The more telling results are the onset and the failure of random moves.

\subsection{Centroids reveal near misses}
\label{sec:errors}

Some wrong answers are \emph{near misses}: the model gets the open question wrong but picks the right option when the same question is asked as multiple choice. We count a wrong answer as a near miss only if the model picks the right option in at least three of the four option orders. This holds for \ErrPosLlama{} of wrong answers in Llama-3.1-8B, \ErrPosQwen{} in Qwen2.5-7B, \ErrPosGemma{} in Gemma-2-9B and only \ErrPosLlamaSmall{} in Llama-3.2-1B. We call the other wrong answers \emph{gaps}. Can the state tell the two apart, and can the model's confidence?

For each wrong answer to a plain test question, we computed two numbers. The first is the \emph{rank of the right centroid}. We measure it at the onset layer, the first layer at which answers are readable. Later layers suit this less, because there nearly every state lies nearest the centroid of the answer the model gave. There, we rank the centroids of the question's kind by their distance from the state, 1 being the nearest, and note where the right answer's centroid falls. The second is the model's \emph{confidence}: its average log-probability per token of its own reply. We measure how well each number separates near misses from gaps by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). This is the probability that a randomly chosen near miss scores better than a randomly chosen gap; 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect. Neither number is fitted to the data.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_recognition.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Telling near misses from gaps.} AUC of each number among wrong answers. The axis starts at chance (0.5), so Gemma-2-9B's confidence (0.49) has no bar.}
\label{fig:errors}
\end{figure}

The rank of the right centroid separates near misses from gaps better than confidence in every model (Figure~\ref{fig:errors}): AUC \AucRankMin{}--\AucRankMax{}, against \AucConfMeanMin{}--\AucConfMeanMax{}. The 95\% interval for the difference excludes zero in every model. For near misses, the right answer's centroid has a median rank of \RankRecogMin{}--\RankRecogMax{}, depending on the model; for gaps, \RankNotMin{}--\RankNotMax{}. A near miss is thus visible inside the model: the right answer's centroid is among the nearest to the state, often just behind the centroid of the answer the model gave. The model's confidence barely shows this. The result is not an effect of the kind of answer. Kinds differ in their share of near misses and their number of candidates, so we repeated the test within countries, the largest kind. The rank still beats confidence in every model: AUC \CountryAucRankMin{}--\CountryAucRankMax{}, against \CountryAucConfMin{}--\CountryAucConfMax{}.
