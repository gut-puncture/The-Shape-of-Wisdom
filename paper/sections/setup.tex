\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

This section covers what every result relies on: the questions, the models and their recorded states, the answer centroids, and readability. Each result in Section~\ref{sec:results} then describes its own test and controls. Details needed only for reproduction are in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\subsection{Questions}
\label{sec:questions}
To see whether questions with the same answer reach the same state, we need many different questions for each answer. We built such a set from Wikidata~\citep{vrandecic2014wikidata}: \NFacts{} facts with 62 possible answers of four kinds, namely 24 countries, 16 cities, 12 languages and 10 sports. The facts come in eight types, which Wikidata calls \emph{relations} (Table~\ref{tab:relations}). Several relations share a kind of answer. A country, for example, can answer a question about a person's citizenship, about where a town is, or about where a film is from. The subjects range from famous to obscure, so the models know some facts and not others.

\begin{table}[t]

\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Answer & Relation & Plain question \\
\midrule
Country & Citizenship & What country is $X$ a citizen of? \\
Country & Town's country & In which country is $X$? \\
Country & Film's country & Which country is the film $X$ from? \\
City & Birthplace & In which city was $X$ born? \\
City & Headquarters & In which city is $X$ headquartered? \\

Language & Film's language & What is the original language of the film $X$? \\

\end{tabular}
\caption{\textbf{The eight relations.} $X$ is the subject: a person, town, film, company or athlete.}
\label{tab:relations}
\end{table}

Each fact is asked as an open question in up to three wordings: the plain question of Table~\ref{tab:relations} and two rewordings. A language model wrote the rewordings in different styles and was never told the answer. For example, a casual rewording of the plain question ``What country is An\'{i}bal Ponce a citizen of?'' is ``Hi, I'd appreciate knowing which country An\'{i}bal Ponce is a citizen of, if possible?'' The plain question is also asked as multiple choice with four options; this format is used only in Section~\ref{sec:errors}.

\subsection{Models and states}
\label{sec:models}
We study four instruction-tuned models: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct~\citep{grattafiori2024the}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct~\citep{qwen2024qwen25}, Gemma-2-9B-it~\citep{gemma2024gemma} and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct. Each answers greedily. At the last position of the prompt, where the answer is produced, we record the residual stream after every layer. We call this vector the question's \emph{state} at that layer. \emph{Depth} is the fraction of the model's layers that the state has passed through, from 0 at the input to 1 at the output.

A reply is right or wrong if it names exactly one answer; replies that hedge, refuse or name two answers are set aside (\UnclearMin{}--\UnclearMax{} of replies, depending on the model).

\subsection{Answer centroids}
\label{sec:places}
An answer's \emph{centroid} at a layer is the average state, at that layer, of the questions to which the model gave that answer:
\begin{equation}
c_a = \frac{1}{|Q_a|} \sum_{q \in Q_a} h_q,
\label{eq:place}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item $a$ is an answer, for example Argentina;
\item $Q_a$ is the set of training questions (defined below) that the model answered with $a$, and $|Q_a|$ is their number;
\item $h_q$ is the state of question $q$ at the layer;
\item $c_a$ is the centroid of $a$ at that layer.
\end{itemize}
For example, the centroid of Argentina at layer 15 of Llama-3.1-8B is the average layer-15 state of all training questions that Llama answered with ``Argentina''. These questions may be about a person, a town or a film, in any wording. Centroids are built from the answers the model gives, not from the true answers. A question that the model wrongly answers with ``Chile'' counts toward the centroid of Chile, so wrong answers can be studied in the same way as right ones.

Centroids are built from questions about one half of the subjects, the \emph{training questions}. Every number we report is measured on questions about the other half, the \emph{test questions}. We split by subject, not by question, because the several questions about one subject are near copies of each other.

\subsection{Readability}
\label{sec:read}
Readability measures how often the answer a model gave can be identified from its state alone. For each test question, we find the centroid nearest to its state, among answers of the same kind:
\begin{equation}
\hat{a}_q = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{a \in A_q} \big\lVert W(h_q) - W(c_a) \big\rVert,
\label{eq:read}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item $h_q$ is the state of test question $q$ at the layer;
\item $A_q$ is the set of answers of the same kind as the answer the model gave to $q$, for example the 24 countries for a country question;
\item $c_a$ is the centroid of answer $a$ at the same layer;
\item $W$ is a whitening, described below;
\item $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ is the ordinary straight-line distance;
\item $\hat{a}_q$ is the answer whose centroid is nearest.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Readability} at a layer is the share of test questions for which $\hat{a}_q$ is the answer the model actually gave. If states carried no information about answers, readability would be about one over the number of candidate answers: 4\% for countries, 10\% for sports.

Distances are measured after whitening because a few dimensions of the residual stream take much larger values than the rest and would otherwise decide every distance. The whitening $W$ keeps the 128 principal components of the training states at that layer (found by principal component analysis, PCA) and rescales each to unit variance, so that every direction counts equally.
