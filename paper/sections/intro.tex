\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask a language model ``What country is An\'{i}bal Ponce a citizen of?'' and, a few dozen layers later, it answers ``Argentina''. On the way, the model's internal state at the last position of the prompt, from which the answer is produced, changes at every layer.

Earlier work reads such intermediate states through the output vocabulary~\citep{nostalgebraist2020interpreting,belrose2023eliciting}, or locates where in the network a fact is retrieved~\citep{geva2023dissecting,meng2022locating}. We ask a geometric question instead. What shape does the change of state take, and do questions with the same answer end up in the same place?

In the four models we study, they do. As a question passes through the network, its state moves into a region that it shares with every other question to which the model gives the same answer. Figure~\ref{fig:hero} shows this in Llama-3.1-8B. Early in the network, questions whose answers are twelve different countries form one tangled cloud. Within a few layers, most of them separate into one strand per country.

To study this, we built \NFacts{} factual questions from Wikidata whose answers recur: 62 countries, cities, languages and sports, each the answer to many questions. We asked them of four open models and recorded the state at every layer. For each answer and layer, we computed the \emph{answer centroid}: the average state of all questions to which the model gave that answer. We then measured, layer by layer, how often a question's state lies nearest the centroid of its own answer. We also tested what the centroids encode, and moved states between centroids to see whether the model uses them. We find the following.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \emph{The answer becomes readable in a single layer} (Section~\ref{sec:step}). In each model, the share of questions whose state lies nearest its own answer's centroid jumps within one layer. We call it the onset layer. Its depth differs between models.
\item \emph{Centroids follow the model's answer, not the question} (Section~\ref{sec:shared}). A country's centroid learned from questions about people also identifies that country in questions about towns and films. It works just as well when the question is reworded, for example as a casual message. It also survives removing the directions the model uses to write the answer's name, so it is more than the model preparing to output that word. When two models give different answers, each model's state follows its own answer.
\item \emph{The model uses the centroids} (Section~\ref{sec:steer}). After the jump, moving a state to an answer's centroid makes the model give that answer; random moves of the same size rarely do. Before the jump, such moves almost never give the right answer, even when they are as large as moves that work after it.
\item \emph{Near misses are visible from the onset} (Section~\ref{sec:errors}). Some wrong answers are near misses: shown the options, the model picks the right one. In a near miss, the right answer's centroid is already among the nearest to the state at the onset layer. This tells near misses apart from real gaps in knowledge about as well as the model's final output does, and better than its confidence in its reply.
\end{itemize}
